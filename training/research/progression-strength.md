# Strength Progression: What 10 Years Could Look Like

*Research brief for Saumya, 12 October 2026. Covers how trained lifters progress year by year, how much people differ, what calorie deficits and concurrent endurance training cost, and a 10-year e1RM projection (age 22 → 32) for bench, squat and deadlift built from his Hevy log and lifting history. Every number has a link. Lines marked "my arithmetic" or "estimate" are calculations or judgement, not cited facts. Builds on `hybrid-goals-research.md` §1, §2 and §5; it doesn't repeat them.*

---

## Executive summary

1. **He's an intermediate, not a novice.** He lifted "somewhat aimlessly" for about 4–5 years, then trained seriously from Nov 2025. Bench e1RM sat at **91–93 kg from March to September 2026** through a calorie deficit, then reached **96 (70×11, Oct 14)** back at maintenance. Novice-style +30–50% years are behind him on bench.
2. **Gains shrink as strength rises.** Among 1,897 competitive powerlifters over 15 years, the strongest quartile of men gained strength at **about half the rate** of the weakest (0.102 vs 0.211 kg/day on the competition total) ([Latella 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7448836/)). World-championship lifters improved **12% (SD 10%)** over the 5 years before peak, and peak age was **35 ± 7** ([Solberg 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30958059/)).
3. **Even untrained people plateau.** On minimal-dose training (14,690 people), strength rose **~30–50% in year 1**. It "practically plateaued" by 1–2 years and was about 50–60% above baseline after 6 years ([Steele 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35591809/)).
4. **People differ a lot.**
   - Over 12 weeks, 1RM gains ranged from **0 to +250%** ([Hubal 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947721/)).
   - In another sample, strength rose 21 ± 11.5% (range −8 to +60%); 12% were high responders and 7% low ([Ahtiainen 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5005877/)).
5. **A deficit slows muscle gain more than strength gain.** In a meta-analysis, a calorie deficit impaired lean-mass gains (ES −0.57) but not strength gains (ES −0.31, not significant). A deficit of about 500 kcal/day prevented lean-mass gain ([Murphy & Koehler 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/)). Over years, strength follows muscle, so long deficits cap the ceiling.
6. **Muscle memory is real, which helps his squat and deadlift.** Strength lost during detraining stays above pre-training levels and comes back fast ([Staron 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1827108/)). Myonuclei persist through detraining, and previously trained muscle regrew larger fibres ([Cumming 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39159314/)). His old squat of about 120 and deadlift of about 150 should come back within months, not years.
7. **Running costs the most on lower-body strength.** Males show blunted lower-body strength gains under concurrent training (SMD −0.43). Keeping sessions more than 2–3 hours apart removes most of the effect (see `hybrid-goals-research.md` §1). Bench is barely affected.

## 2. Evidence

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| [Latella 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7448836/) (n=1,897, 2003–18) | Men's total strength gain 0.15 ± 0.44 kg/day (extrapolated ~55 kg/yr on a mean total of ~513 kg). Mean first-to-best gain in men about +61 kg (~12%, my arithmetic). Strongest quartile: half the rate of the weakest. |
| [Solberg 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30958059/) (world champs, 2003–17) | Peak age: powerlifting **35 (7)**, weightlifting 26 (3). 5-year improvement before peak: **12% (10%)** in powerlifting. |
| Renner 2026, via `hybrid-goals-research.md` | Classic lifters: cohort peak about 27; individuals peak 4–6 years after they start competing. |
| [Steele 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35591809/) (n=14,690, up to 6.8 y, 1×/week minimal dose) | Linear-log growth: ~30–50% in year 1, plateau by ~1–2 years, ~50–60% above baseline at 6 years. |
| [Rhea 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12618576/) (140 studies) | Best gains: untrained at ~60% 1RM and 3 days/week per muscle; trained at ~80% 1RM and 2 days/week; 4 sets per muscle group for both. |
| [Hubal 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947721/) (n=585) | 12 weeks: 1RM gains 0 to +250%; size −2 to +59%. |
| [Ahtiainen 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5005877/) (n=287) | Strength +21.1 ± 11.5% (−8 to +60%); 12% high responders, 7% low; age and sex made no difference. |
| [Murphy & Koehler 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/) | Deficit: lean mass ES −0.57 (p = 0.02); strength ES −0.31 (not significant). ~500 kcal/day deficit prevented lean-mass gain. |
| [Staron 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1827108/) | After 30–32 weeks off, strength dropped but not to pre-training levels; 6 weeks of retraining restored fibre size quickly. |
| [Cumming 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39159314/) | Myonuclei kept through 16 weeks of detraining; after retraining, the previously trained muscle had larger type II fibres than the untrained control. |
| [Kouri 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7496846/) (n=157) | Drug-free normalised FFMI topped out at about 25.0. |

## 3. Conflicts and weak evidence

- **A citation in an existing file couldn't be verified.** `hybrid-goals-research.md` and `periodisation-plan.md` cite Latella 2020 as "7.5–12.5% in year one, ~20% after 10 years". Those figures **aren't in the parts of the paper I could access** (abstract and PMC text). The paper reports gain per day and by quartile. Treat the "20% over 10 years" as **not verified** until someone finds it in a figure.
- **Powerlifting data covers competitors**, who are self-selected and often specialised. A hybrid athlete with heavy running volume should expect *less* than those numbers on squat and deadlift.
- **Steele 2023 was minimal-dose, untrained, average age 48.** It shows the *shape* of the curve (log-linear, early plateau), not his rate.
- **Muscle-memory studies are short** (weeks to months) and use small samples. "Back to 120/150 in 6–12 months" is an **estimate**.
- **Old maxes are from memory.** The 120 squat and 150 deadlift are self-reported and from years ago.

## 4. Evidence gaps

- No long-term (5–10 year) data on **hybrid** athletes' strength progression while doing endurance volume.
- No research-based year-by-year muscle-gain model for trained lifters. Lyle McDonald, Alan Aragon and Casey Butt's models are heuristics from coaches.
- No recent tested 1RMs for him. The squat and deadlift baselines are estimates until he tests them.

## 6. What this means for Saumya

- **Bench 100 kg:** from e1RM 96 it's +4%. The smooth median curve reaches ~98 by Feb 2027 and ~102 by Oct 2027. A focused Dec–Feb block in a surplus makes **100 in February the likely outcome**, with 102.5–105 as the reach. A true 1RM usually tests a little below a high-rep e1RM.
- **Squat and deadlift are the cheapest wins.** Getting back to 120 and 150 is relearning, not new adaptation, and should take months. After that they sit in the lane most exposed to running interference. Keep heavy lower-body work on days well separated from runs; the new Upper/Upper/Lower layout already does this.
- **Long-term ceiling:** the median is bench ~115, squat ~140, deadlift ~180 by 30–32. Reaching "advanced" standards across the board takes the fast curve *and* dedicated strength blocks in the off-season.
- **What to measure:** test 1RMs (or rep maxes) at the end of each strength block, and re-baseline squat and deadlift after the first Lower block.
