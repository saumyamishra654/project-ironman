How to read the three curves

All three assume the same thing: you keep training consistently on your current hybrid plan, with no long breaks. What changes is how strongly your body responds. In the big training studies, people on identical programmes ranged from "almost nothing" to more than double the average gain. About half of that spread is genetic.

  • Year one is front-loaded. Untrained adults gained a median 16% VO₂max in a year, with half of it in the first 3 months. On a constant dose, nothing changed after month 6 (Scharhag-Rosenberger 2009). You're about 8 months in, which is why the curves bend now.
  • More dose restarts it. Every "non-responder" improved once training rose by about 2 h/week (Montero & Lundby 2017).
  • Later gains come from threshold and economy. A world-class runner got 8% faster while her VO₂max fell, because her threshold speed rose 20% (Jones 1998). Runners doing over 100 km/week were 19% faster at the same %VO₂max, purely through economy (Scrimgeour 1986).
  • The tail is small. World top-100 distance runners improve only 3–5% across their last five years before peaking (Haugen 2018). There's no age decline before about 35 (Tanaka & Seals 2008).

  • Gains shrink as you get stronger. Among 1,897 powerlifters, the strongest men gained at about half the rate of the weakest (Latella 2020). World-level lifters added about 12% in their last five years before peaking, around age 35 (Solberg 2019).
  • Responses vary wildly. Over 12 weeks, 1RM gains ranged from 0 to +250% (Hubal 2005). In a second sample, 12% were high responders and 7% low (Ahtiainen 2016).
  • A deficit stalls muscle more than strength. Strength gains held up in a deficit, but lean-mass gains didn't. A deficit of about 500 kcal/day stopped them (Murphy & Koehler 2022). Over years, strength follows muscle.
  • Old strength comes back fast because the extra nuclei your muscle fibres built persist through time off (Staron 1991; Cumming 2024). Running costs the most on lower-body gains in men, unless sessions are kept hours apart.

  • The bike decides a 70.3. It's 50–60% of race time and the best single predictor of the total. FTP alone explained 64% of bike-split variance (70.3 systematic review, 2026). It's also your least developed leg, so it's your biggest lever.
  • Fitness doesn't fully carry across sports. Runners hit a lower VO₂max on a bike than on a treadmill (Millet 2009). Novices respond strongly, though: about +17% in 20 weeks on average (HERITAGE). Trained riders on about 8 h/week sit near 4.2 W/kg, and hours explain most of the gap (r = 0.86).
  • Swimming is mostly regaining. Long breaks erase recent gains but leave a floor above untrained (Mujika & Padilla 2000), and your technique is intact. Masters swimmers don't decline until 35–40. A wetsuit (Norseman) is worth 3–5% (Cordain 1991).

What moves you between curves

Genetics sets the band. These decide where in it you land.

Dose

≈ +1 band

Progressive volume beats a fixed routine. Non-response disappears with about 2 h/week more training.

Run volume

50–80 km/wk blocks

Weekly km and training pace explain about 77% of marathon-time variance (Tanda 2011). The fast-curve goals need run-focused blocks.

Race weight

80 → 76 kg of fat raises VO₂max per kg by about 5% (my arithmetic). That's the upper bound.

How much to trust this

The research is solid on year one and on elite athletes. The years in between (2–6) for adults who start late are inferred, not measured. These are bands for calibrating expectations, not predictions.
