# Running Progression: What 10 Years Could Look Like

*Research brief for Saumya, 12 October 2026. Covers how adult-onset runners improve year by year, how much people differ, and a 10-year pace projection (age 22 → 32) built from his actual sessions rather than the Garmin VO₂max. Every number has a link. Lines marked "my arithmetic" or "estimate" are calculations or judgement, not cited facts. Builds on `hybrid-goals-research.md` §2 and §4; it doesn't repeat them.*

---

## Executive summary

1. **Year one is fast, then it decelerates.** In a 1-year study of previously untrained adults, VO₂max rose a median **16% (IQR 9–20%)**. Half of that came in the first 3 months, 79% by month 9. On a constant stimulus there was no further change after 6 months ([Scharhag-Rosenberger 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19346973/)).
2. **Raising the dose gets gains moving again.** Every "non-responder" responded once training rose by 120 min/week ([Montero & Lundby 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5451738/)). A strenuous 10-week programme produced a linear **+44%** VO₂max ([Hickson 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/838658/)).
3. **People differ enormously.** On the same 20-week programme, mean VO₂max gain was about 400 mL/min. Some people gained "little or no" VO₂max; others gained more than 1.0 L/min. Heritability of the response was 47% ([HERITAGE, Bouchard 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10484570/)).
4. **After year 1–2, gains come from threshold and economy, not VO₂max.**
   - A world-class runner improved 3000 m by 8% while her VO₂max *fell* 73 → 66. Her lactate threshold rose 15 → 18 km/h, and her oxygen cost at 16 km/h dropped 53 → 48 mL/kg/min ([Jones 1998](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1756052/)).
   - Paula Radcliffe's economy improved about 15% over 11 years with VO₂max flat ([Jones 2006](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249883653_The_Physiology_of_the_World_Record_Holder_for_the_Women's_Marathon)).
5. **Volume drives the long tail.**
   - Runners doing more than 100 km/week were 19.2% faster than those doing less than 100 km/week, at the same %VO₂max. The difference was explained by economy (19.9% better) ([Scrimgeour 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3699009/)).
   - Weekly distance and training pace explain about 77% of the variance in marathon time ([Tanda 2011](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262686102_Prediction_of_marathon_performance_time_on_the_basis_of_training_indices)). A meta-regression of 85 studies agrees ([Doherty 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31704026/)).
6. **Near the ceiling, improvement is tiny.** World top-100 distance runners improve only 3.4–5.2% in total over the 5 years before peak ([Haugen 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29543080/)).
7. **Age is not a factor before 32.** Peak endurance performance holds to about 35, and economy doesn't decline with age in trained adults ([Tanaka & Seals 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2375571/)).
8. **His own data:** VDOT went from about 28 in March to about 37 now. That's about +26% speed in 7 months, well above the 15.8% average for new parkrunners ([Stevinson & Hickson 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23954885/)). His response so far is at the high end.

## 2. Evidence

### Year 1: how fast and how front-loaded

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| [Scharhag-Rosenberger 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19346973/) (n=18 untrained, 42 y, jog 3×45 min/wk at 60% HRR for 12 months) | VO₂max +0.36 L/min, median **+16% (IQR 9–20%)**. 52% / 65% / 79% of the gain by months 3 / 6 / 9. Submaximal HR −11 bpm, 93% of it by month 3. Authors: increase the stimulus after 6 months, because nothing more changed after that on a constant dose. |
| [Scharhag-Rosenberger 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20561283/) (same cohort) | 8 of 18 improved on every variable. In 10, one or two variables didn't move (VO₂max unchanged in 4). Every subject improved on at least one variable. |
| [Bouchard 1999, HERITAGE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10484570/) (n=481, 20 wk cycle training) | Mean VO₂max +~400 mL/min; range "little or no gain" to >1.0 L/min. 2.5× more variance between families than within; heritability **47%**. |
| [Barber 2022, HERITAGE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8380259/) | Being a high or low responder on one trait doesn't predict another; 49% had at least one high and one low response. |
| [Montero & Lundby 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5451738/) (n=78) | Non-response fell as weekly dose rose from 60 to 300 min. Adding 120 min/week abolished non-response in every case. |
| [Hickson 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/838658/) | 40 min/day, 6 days/week of strenuous intervals and running: VO₂max **+44%** in 10 weeks, rising linearly. |
| [Stevinson & Hickson 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23954885/) (parkrun, n=7,308) | 5K performance: initial non-runners **+15.8%**, everyone else **+10.2%**. |

### Years 2–10: threshold, economy, volume

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| [Joyner & Coyle 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2375555/) | Performance velocity = VO₂max × the fraction you can sustain (threshold) ÷ oxygen cost. Lactate threshold: about 60% of VO₂max untrained, 75–85% trained, 75–90% elite. Running economy varies 30–40% between individuals. |
| [Jones 1998](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1756052/) (world-class runner, 1991–95) | 3000 m +8% in 2 years, then stable. VO₂max 73 → 66, but lactate threshold 15 → 18 km/h (+20%) and VO₂ at 16 km/h 53 → 48 (−9.4%). |
| [Jones 2006](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249883653_The_Physiology_of_the_World_Record_Holder_for_the_Women's_Marathon) (Radcliffe, via summary) | Economy +~15% from 1992 to 2003, VO₂max about 70 throughout. |
| [Scrimgeour 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3699009/) (n=30) | Over 100 km/week: 19.2% faster; same %VO₂max; economy 19.9% better. It's cross-sectional, so trained-in vs inherited can't be separated. |
| [Tanda 2011](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262686102_Prediction_of_marathon_performance_time_on_the_basis_of_training_indices) | Marathon pace (s/km) = 17.1 + 140·e^(−0.0053·km/week) + 0.55·training pace. About 77% of variance explained (n=22, 46 races). |
| [Doherty 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31704026/) (85 studies, 137 cohorts) | Weekly distance, runs per week, longest run and training pace all predict marathon time (R² 0.38–0.81). |
| [Emig & Peltonen 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7538888/) (~14,000 runners, 1.6M sessions) | Real-world data supports modelling performance from an aerobic power index plus an endurance index. Both correlate with training volume and intensity. |
| [Haugen 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29543080/) (14,937 world top-100 athletes) | Peak at 25–27 (marathon about 28–29). Distance runners improved only **3.4–5.2%** over the last 5 years before peak. |
| [Tanaka & Seals 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2375571/) | Peak endurance performance holds to about 35, then declines modestly. Economy doesn't change with age in trained adults. |

### Body mass

- Adding 7.5% external weight measurably slowed trained runners' treadmill and 12-minute run performance ([Cureton 1980](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7421479/)).
- **My arithmetic:** with absolute VO₂max held constant, dropping from 80 to 76 kg of fat raises VO₂max per kg by about 5%. That's roughly the upper bound on the speed gain. The real gain is smaller if lean mass is lost too.

## 3. Conflicts and weak evidence

- **No peer-reviewed year-by-year curve exists for adult-onset recreational runners after year 1.** Year 1 is well studied, and so is the near-ceiling elite end. The middle (years 2–6) is inferred from mechanism studies and cross-sectional volume data.
- The detailed long-term physiology comes mostly from **case studies of elite women** (Jones 1998, 2006). The direction carries over; the exact magnitudes may not.
- **HERITAGE was cycle training**, and Scharhag-Rosenberger used a constant HR dose. Neither represents a progressive running plan.
- **Riegel/VDOT equivalents run optimistic for the marathon** (Vickers 2016, cited in `hybrid-goals-research.md`). Projected marathon times assume real marathon-specific training.
- **Triathlon and lifting dilute running volume.** No study quantifies the multi-year cost of that for running. The median scenario below assumes hybrid volume (about 35–50 km/week).

## 4. Evidence gaps

- No longitudinal data on adult-onset men aged 22–32 that tracks performance by training year.
- No direct data on how much concurrent swim, bike and lifting slows running progression over years.
- No measurement of his lactate threshold as a fraction of VO₂max, or of his economy. A lab test would replace three of the model's assumptions with numbers.

## 6. What this means for Saumya

- **His response so far is at the high end.** About +26% speed in 7 months beats the 15.8% parkrun average for non-runners. The slow curve is what happens if gains stall early or training gets interrupted.
- **The 95th-percentile targets (HM sub-1:25, marathon sub-3, 5K sub-19) are on the fast curve.** It crosses HM 1:25 and marathon 3:00 around 27–30, and 5K sub-19 around 26. The median curve lands at HM ~1:34–1:36. Race weight (up to ~5%) and run-focused blocks of 50–80 km/week are what move a median responder toward the fast curve.
- **The biggest single change is years 1–3.** Even the median curve gets about 75% of its 10-year gain by age 25. That's the window where consistency matters most.
- **What to measure to narrow the bands:** a clean HM on Dec 20; a 5K or 30-minute test each spring and autumn; and, if possible, a lab lactate/VO₂max test to measure the threshold fraction and economy directly.
